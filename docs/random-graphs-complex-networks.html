Chapter 10 Random Graphs & Complex Networks

10.1 The Erdős–Rényi Random Graph Models

Random graph theory studies the properties of graphs generated by probabilistic processes. Introduced by Paul Erdős and Alfréd Rényi in the late 1950s, these models serve as baseline structures for analyzing average-case algorithm performance and network connectivity.

10.1.1 Model Formulations

  1. The \(G(n, M)\) Model: A fixed number of \(n\) vertices and exactly \(M\) edges are chosen uniformly at random from the set of all \(\binom{\binom{n}{2}}{M}\) possible simple graphs.
  2. The \(G(n, p)\) Model: A graph on \(n\) labeled vertices where each of the possible \(\binom{n}{2}\) edges is included independently with probability \(p \in [0, 1]\).
                     G(n, p) Edge Inclusion
               (1)                   (1)
              .   .                 /   \       Prob = p (Included)
             .     .     =====>    /     \      
            .       .             .       .     Prob = 1 - p (Omitted)
           (2).....(3)           (2)     (3)

10.1.1.1 Basic Statistics of \(G(n, p)\)

  • Expected Number of Edges:

\[\mathbb{E}[\vert{}E\vert{}] = p \binom{n}{2} = \frac{p \cdot n(n-1)}{2}\]

  • Expected Degree of a Vertex:

\[\mathbb{E}[d(v)] = (n - 1)p\]

  • Degree Distribution: The degree of any vertex follows a Binomial distribution \(d(v) \sim \text{Binom}(n - 1, p)\). For large \(n\) and constant expected degree \(\lambda = np\), this converges to a Poisson distribution:

\[P(d(v) = k) \approx e^{-\lambda} \frac{\lambda^k}{k!}\]

10.2 Phase Transitions & Threshold Functions

One of the defining discoveries of random graph theory is that structural properties emerge suddenly as the edge probability \(p(n)\) crosses critical threshold functions.

10.2.1 Asymptotic Almost Surely (a.a.s.)

A graph property \(P\) holds asymptotically almost surely (a.a.s.) in \(G(n, p)\) if the probability that \(G(n, p)\) satisfies \(P\) approaches \(1\) as \(n \to \infty\):

\[\lim_{n \to \infty} \mathbb{P}(G(n, p) \in P) = 1\]

10.2.2 Definition of Threshold Function

A function \(p_c(n)\) is a threshold function for a graph property \(P\) if:

\[\lim_{n \to \infty} \mathbb{P}(G(n, p) \in P) = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if } p(n) \ll p_c(n) \\ 1 & \text{if } p(n) \gg p_c(n) \end{cases}\]

                Phase Transition Threshold (p_c)
       P(Property holds)
             1.0 |                   /-----------
                 |                  /
                 |                 /
             0.0 +----------------+-------------> p(n)
                                p_c(n)

10.2.3 Classical Evolution of \(G(n, p)\)

As \(p(n)\) increases, the global structure of \(G(n, p)\) undergoes distinct phase transitions:

Edge Probability \(p(n)\) Expected Degree \(\lambda = np\) Structural Emergence / Phase
\(p(n) = o(1/n)\) \(\lambda \to 0\) Subcritical Phase: Small isolated components (trees of size \(O(\log n)\)).
\(p(n) = 1/n\) \(\lambda = 1\) Critical Window: A Giant Component of size \(O(n^{2/3})\) emerges.
\(p(n) = c/n \ (c > 1)\) \(\lambda = c > 1\) Supercritical Phase: Unique Giant Component containing \(\Theta(n)\) vertices.
\(p(n) = \frac{\ln n}{n}\) \(\lambda = \ln n\) Connectivity Phase: Disappearance of last isolated vertex; \(G(n, p)\) becomes connected a.a.s.

10.3 Complex Networks & Real-World Topologies

While Erdős–Rényi graphs provide strong mathematical guarantees, real-world networks (social networks, biological pathways, the World Wide Web) exhibit structural traits that random \(G(n, p)\) models fail to capture.

       Random Graph (Poisson)                 Scale-Free Network (Power Law)
       - Homogeneous degrees                  - Heterogeneous: High-degree hubs
       - Low clustering                       - High clustering + Small-world
              (o)                                     ( HUB )
             / | \                                   / / | \ \
           (o)-(o)-(o)                            (o)(o)(o)(o)(o)

10.3.1 Structural Metrics of Real-World Networks

  1. Degree Distribution Heavy Tails: Real networks contain structural “hubs”—a small number of vertices with extraordinarily high degree.
  2. High Clustering Coefficient: Neighboring nodes in social networks are much more likely to be connected to each other than in random graphs.
  3. Small-World Phenomenon: Short average path lengths scale logarithmically with graph size (\(L \propto \log n\)).

10.4 Scale-Free Networks & Small-World Models

To bridge the gap between pure random graphs and real-world networks, several generative network models were developed.

10.4.1 Watts–Strogatz Small-World Model (1998)

The Watts–Strogatz model generates graphs with both high clustering and short path lengths.

10.4.1.1 Construction Steps

  1. Start with a regular ring lattice of \(n\) vertices where each vertex is connected to its \(k\) nearest neighbors.
  2. For every edge, re-wire its target vertex with probability \(p_{\text{rewire}}\) to a randomly selected node.
      Ring Lattice (p = 0)        Small World (0 < p < 1)         Random Graph (p = 1)
       High Clustering             High Clustering                 Low Clustering
       Long Path Length            Short Path Length               Short Path Length

10.4.2 Barabási–Albert Scale-Free Model (1999)

The Barabási–Albert model generates networks with a power-law degree distribution using preferential attachment.

10.4.2.1 Power-Law Degree Distribution

\[P(k) \propto k^{-\gamma}\]

(where \(\gamma\) typically ranges between \(2\) and \(3\) in physical and social systems).

10.4.2.2 Construction Steps

  1. Growth: Start with a small seed graph of \(m_0\) vertices. At each time step, add a new vertex with \(m \le m_0\) edges.
  2. Preferential Attachment: The probability \(\Pi(v_i)\) that the new vertex connects to an existing vertex \(v_i\) is proportional to \(v_i\)’s degree \(d(v_i)\):

\[\Pi(v_i) = \frac{d(v_i)}{\sum_{j} d(v_j)}\]
