14.1.1 Question 1: Fundamentals, Trees & Structural Properties

14.1.1.1 1. Odd Degree Vertices (Handshaking Lemma)

  • Proof: By the Handshaking Lemma, the sum of all vertex degrees in a graph \(G = (V, E)\) equals twice the number of edges:

\[\sum_{v \in V} \deg(v) = 2\vert{}E\vert{}\]

  • Partition \(V\) into two sets: \(V_{\text{even}}\) (vertices with even degree) and \(V_{\text{odd}}\) (vertices with odd degree):

\[\sum_{v \in V_{\text{even}}} \deg(v) + \sum_{v \in V_{\text{odd}}} \deg(v) = 2\vert{}E\vert{}\]

  • The term \(\sum_{v \in V_{\text{even}}} \deg(v)\) is even because it is a sum of even integers. The right-hand side \(2\vert{}E\vert{}\) is also even.
  • Therefore, \(\sum_{v \in V_{\text{odd}}} \deg(v) = 2\vert{}E\vert{} - \sum_{v \in V_{\text{even}}} \deg(v)\) must be an even integer.
  • Since the sum of odd integers can only be even if there is an even number of terms, the cardinality \(\vert{}V_{\text{odd}}\vert{}\) must be even. \(\blacksquare\)

14.1.1.2 2. Minimum Two Leaves in a Tree

  • Proof: Let \(T = (V, E)\) be a tree with \(\vert{}V\vert{} = n \ge 2\).
  • By definition of a tree, \(\vert{}E\vert{} = n - 1\). Applying the Handshaking Lemma:

\[\sum_{v \in V} \deg(v) = 2\vert{}E\vert{} = 2(n - 1) = 2n - 2\]

  • Assume for contradiction that \(T\) has fewer than 2 leaves (vertices of degree 1). Since \(T\) is connected and \(n \ge 2\), every vertex has degree at least 1 (\(\deg(v) \ge 1\)).
  • Case 1: 0 leaves. Then \(\deg(v) \ge 2\) for all \(v \in V\):

\[\sum_{v \in V} \deg(v) \ge 2n > 2n - 2\]

Contradiction. * Case 2: 1 leaf (\(v_1\)). Then \(\deg(v_1) = 1\) and \(\deg(v) \ge 2\) for all other \(n - 1\) vertices:

\[\sum_{v \in V} \deg(v) \ge 1 + 2(n - 1) = 2n - 1 > 2n - 2\]

Contradiction. * Hence, \(T\) must contain at least 2 vertices of degree 1 (leaves). \(\blacksquare\)

14.1.1.3 3. Labeled Spanning Trees of \(K_6\)

  • Cayley’s Formula: \(n^{n-2}\). For \(n = 6\):

\[6^{6-2} = 6^4 = 1296 \text{ labeled spanning trees.}\]

  • Non-Isomorphic Trees:
  1. Path Graph \(P_6\): A linear chain \((1-2-3-4-5-6)\) with diameter 5 and maximum degree 2.
  2. Star Graph \(K_{1,5}\): A central node connected to 5 leaf nodes, with diameter 2 and maximum degree 5.

14.1.2 Question 2: Traversability, Planarity & Colorings

14.1.2.1 1. Eulerian Circuit Conditions & Necessity Proof

  • Theorem: A connected graph \(G\) contains an Eulerian circuit if and only if every vertex in \(G\) has an even degree.
  • Necessity Proof:
  • Let \(C\) be an Eulerian circuit in \(G\).
  • For every vertex \(v \in V\), each time \(C\) enters \(v\) along an edge, it must leave \(v\) along a distinct, previously unvisited edge.
  • Thus, every arrival and departure contributes \(2\) to the degree of \(v\).
  • Since \(C\) traverses every edge in \(E\) exactly once and starts/ends at the same vertex, the total degree of every vertex must be even. \(\blacksquare\)

14.1.2.2 2. Euler’s Formula & Edge Upper Bound

  • Euler’s Formula: For any connected planar graph with \(V\) vertices, \(E\) edges, and \(F\) faces:

\[V - E + F = 2\]

  • Proof of \(\vert{}E\vert{} \le 3V - 6\):
  • Each face is bounded by at least 3 edges in a simple planar graph.
  • Since each edge borders at most 2 faces:

\[2E = \sum_{f \in \text{Faces}} \text{deg}(f) \ge 3F \implies F \le \frac{2}{3}E\]

  • Substitute into Euler’s formula:

\[V - E + \frac{2}{3}E \ge 2 \implies V - \frac{1}{3}E \ge 2 \implies E \le 3V - 6 \quad \blacksquare\]

14.1.2.3 3. Non-Planarity of \(K_{3,3}\)

  • \(K_{3,3}\) has \(V = 6\) vertices and \(E = 9\) edges.
  • Because \(K_{3,3}\) is bipartite, it contains no odd cycles (no triangles). Thus, every face must be bounded by at least 4 edges:

\[2E \ge 4F \implies F \le \frac{1}{2}E\]

  • Substituting into Euler’s formula (\(V - E + F = 2\)):

\[V - E + \frac{1}{2}E \ge 2 \implies E \le 2V - 4\]

  • For \(K_{3,3}\), \(2V - 4 = 2(6) - 4 = 8\).
  • Since \(E = 9 > 8\), \(K_{3,3}\) violates the bipartite planar edge bound and is non-planar. \(\blacksquare\)

14.1.3 Question 3: Flows, Connectivity & Matchings

14.1.3.1 1. Max-Flow Min-Cut Application

  • Theorem: \(\max \vert{}f\vert{} = \min c(S, T)\).
  • Upper Bound: If the minimum cut capacity is 14, the maximum possible flow value is 14.

14.1.3.2 2. Hall’s Marriage Theorem Counterexample

  • Hall’s Theorem: A bipartite graph \(G = (A \cup B, E)\) has a matching covering \(A\) iff \(\vert{}N(S)\vert{} \ge \vert{}S\vert{}\) for all \(S \subseteq A\).
  • Failure Example:
  • Let \(A = \{a_1, a_2, a_3, a_4\}\) and \(B = \{b_1, b_2, b_3, b_4\}\).
  • Define edges such that \(a_1, a_2, a_3\) all connect only to \(b_1\):

\[E = \{(a_1, b_1), (a_2, b_1), (a_3, b_1), (a_4, b_2)\}\]

  • Choose subset \(S = \{a_1, a_2, a_3\} \subseteq A\).
  • Neighborhood \(N(S) = \{b_1\}\), so \(\vert{}S\vert{} = 3\) and \(\vert{}N(S)\vert{} = 1\).
  • Since \(\vert{}N(S)\vert{} = 1 < 3 = \vert{}S\vert{}\), Hall’s condition fails and no complete matching exists.

14.1.3.3 3. Bipartite Matching to Network Flow Reduction

  1. Construct directed network \(G' = (V', E')\).
  2. Add super-source \(s\) and super-sink \(t\).
  3. Add directed edges \((s, a)\) with capacity \(1\) for all \(a \in A\).
  4. Direct all original edges from \(A \to B\) with capacity \(1\).
  5. Add directed edges \((b, t)\) with capacity \(1\) for all \(b \in B\).
  6. Solve max flow; maximum flow value \(\vert{}f^*\vert{}\) equals maximum matching size \(\vert{}M^*\vert{}\).

14.1.4 Question 4: Spectral & Algebraic Graph Theory

14.1.4.1 1. Matrices for \(K_4\)

\[A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad D = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\]

\[L = D - A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & -1 & -1 & -1 \\ -1 & 3 & -1 & -1 \\ -1 & -1 & 3 & -1 \\ -1 & -1 & -1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\]

14.1.4.2 2. Proof that 0 is a Laplacian Eigenvalue

  • Every row of \(L = D - A\) sums to zero because \(L_{ii} = d(v_i)\) and \(\sum_{j \neq i} L_{ij} = -d(v_i)\).
  • Consider the all-ones vector \(\mathbf{1} = (1, 1, 1, 1)^T\):

\[(L \mathbf{1})_i = \sum_{j=1}^n L_{ij} \cdot 1 = 0 = 0 \cdot 1_i\]

  • Thus, \(L \mathbf{1} = 0 \cdot \mathbf{1}\), proving \(\mu_1 = 0\) is an eigenvalue with eigenvector \(\mathbf{1}\). \(\blacksquare\)

14.1.4.3 3. Fiedler Value (\(\mu_2\)) Significance

  • \(\mu_2\) measures algebraic connectivity.
  • If \(\mu_2 = 0\), the graph \(G\) has at least 2 connected components (i.e., \(G\) is disconnected).

14.1.4.4 4. \(A^3\) Traces on \(C_4\)

  • For \(C_4\), \(A^3_{ii}\) counts closed walks of length 3 starting and ending at \(v_i\).
  • Because \(C_4\) is bipartite, it contains no odd-length cycles (no 3-cycles).
  • Therefore, \(A^3_{ii} = 0\) for all \(i\), and \(\text{Tr}(A^3) = 0\).

14.1.5 Question 5: Complexity, Approximations & Dynamic Programming

14.1.5.1 1. Complexity Classes

  • P: Problems solvable in polynomial time \(O(n^k)\) (e.g., Shortest Path via Dijkstra).
  • NP: Decision problems verifiable in polynomial time.
  • NP-Complete: Hardest problems in NP (e.g., Hamiltonian Cycle).

14.1.5.2 2. Vertex Cover 2-Approximation Proof

  • Algorithm selects maximal matching \(M \subseteq E\) and takes both endpoints of every edge in \(M\), giving \(\vert{}C\vert{} = 2\vert{}M\vert{}\).
  • Any valid cover \(\text{OPT}\) must pick at least one endpoint from each disjoint matching edge in \(M\), so \(\vert{}\text{OPT}\vert{} \ge \vert{}M\vert{}\).
  • Combining inequalities: \(\vert{}C\vert{} = 2\vert{}M\vert{} \le 2\vert{}\text{OPT}\vert{} \implies \alpha = 2\). \(\blacksquare\)

14.1.5.3 3. Floyd-Warshall Recurrence

\[d_k(i, j) = \min \left( d_{k-1}(i, j), \; d_{k-1}(i, k) + d_{k-1}(k, j) \right)\]

  • Time Complexity: \(O(V^3)\)
  • Space Complexity: \(O(V^2)\)

14.1.5.4 4. Tree Dynamic Programming

  • Root the tree at an arbitrary node.

  • States for node \(u\):

  • \(DP[u][0]\): Max independent set size in \(u\)’s subtree when \(u\) is excluded.

  • \(DP[u][1]\): Max independent set size in \(u\)’s subtree when \(u\) is included.

  • Compute in post-order bottom-up traversal in \(O(V)\) total time.

14.1.6 Question 6: Random Graphs, Networks & Advanced Frontiers

14.1.6.1 1. Erdős–Rényi Expectations

  • Expected Edges: \(\mathbb{E}[\vert{}E\vert{}] = p \binom{n}{2} = \frac{p \cdot n(n-1)}{2}\)
  • Expected Degree: \(\mathbb{E}[d(v)] = p(n - 1)\)

14.1.6.2 2. Degree Distribution Comparison

  • Erdős–Rényi \(G(n, p)\): Binomial/Poisson distribution. Nodes have homogeneous degrees clustered tightly around the mean; large hubs are exponentially unlikely.
  • Barabási–Albert: Power-law distribution \(P(k) \propto k^{-3}\). Scale-free and highly heterogeneous, featuring a small number of ultra-connected hubs.

14.1.6.3 3. Reconstruction Conjecture

  • Conjecture: Every simple graph \(G\) with \(\vert{}V\vert{} \ge 3\) is uniquely determined up to isomorphism by its deck.
  • Deck: The multiset of all \(\vert{}V\vert{}\) unlabeled vertex-deleted subgraphs \(\{G - v : v \in V\}\).
